Melodic dictation — hearing a short melody and writing it down or playing it back — is where [ear training](/learn/what-is-ear-training) stops being a drill and starts being musicianship. It fuses everything else: interval recognition, pitch memory, rhythm. It's also the skill that exposes whether your other practice actually transferred.

## Why it feels harder than everything else

Interval quizzes let you answer one sound at a time, at your own pace. A melody gives you five or ten notes in three seconds and asks for all of them. The difficulty isn't hearing — it's **memory**: holding the phrase in your head long enough to decode it. That's why dictation training is as much about chunking and working memory as about pitch.

The prerequisite, as always, is intervals. If a perfect fourth inside a melody still surprises you, go back to [interval ear training](/learn/interval-ear-training) for a while; dictation on a shaky interval foundation is just guessing with extra steps.

## The multi-pass method

Never try to write the whole melody in one listen. Professional transcribers don't — they take passes, and each pass has one job:

1. **Pass one: just listen.** No writing. Let the melody become a thing you can hum. If you can't hum it, you're not ready to write it.
2. **Pass two: contour.** Sketch the shape — up, down, big leap, step — as a line above the words or staff. Contour is the skeleton; most people can get it on one hearing.
3. **Pass three: rhythm.** Tap or notate the rhythm alone, ignoring pitch. Rhythm is easier to catch in isolation than while you're also chasing notes.
4. **Pass four: anchor notes.** Lock the first note, the last note, and the highest or lowest point. These are your landmarks.
5. **Pass five: fill in with intervals.** Walk between landmarks step by step, naming each interval as you go. Small intervals first; double-check every leap against the contour.

Sing back before you write, at every pass. If your voice can't reproduce it, your ear hasn't caught it — your pencil is just guessing.

## A worked micro-example

Watch the method run on the first phrase of "Mary Had a Little Lamb" — four notes, all small:

1. **Listen.** Hum it. You already can, which is the point of starting with songs you know.
2. **Contour.** Down, down, up, then three repeated notes.
3. **Rhythm.** Even quarter notes — nothing to chase here, which is also the point.
4. **Anchors.** First note and the lowest note: in scale degrees, it starts on 3 and dips to 1.
5. **Fill in.** 3→2 is a step down (major second), 2→1 another step down, 1→2 a step back up. Done: 3 2 1 2, 3 3 3.

That took five passes for four notes. Good — the passes are the skill. As they merge into instinct, the phrases you can handle get longer.

## Build from two notes, not from "Twinkle Twinkle"

Start absurdly short: two-note melodies, stepwise, in one key. When two notes are trivial, go to three. Melodic dictation skill scales with **length and leap size**, so only ever increase one at a time. Most learners plateau because they jump to real songs too early — real melodies have syncopation, chromatic notes, and ten-second phrases. Get to eight-note diatonic phrases first; real songs stop being hard surprisingly soon after that.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing during the first listen.** You split attention and get neither the memory nor the notes. Listen first, always.
- **Never checking by singing.** Singing back is the cheapest accuracy check there is.
- **Practicing only in one key.** Transposition invariance is the goal: the same phrase starting on a different note should be equally easy. Vary the starting pitch.
- **Skipping rhythm.** A dictation with right notes and wrong rhythm is wrong. Give rhythm its own pass.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I write in solfege, scale degrees, or note names?

Whichever you'll actually use — the skill is the same underneath. Movable-do solfege and scale degrees (1 2 3…) transfer best across keys; absolute note names help if your goal is notation on staff.

### How many replays should I allow myself?

While learning the method: as many as you need, one per pass. Once the method is solid, ration them — three listens, then answer — because exams and real transcribing won't wait.

### When am I ready for real songs?

When eight-note diatonic phrases feel comfortable. Start with songs you know by heart (you're training writing, not guessing) and move to unfamiliar ones after.

### Does singing ability limit my dictation?

Only your _inner_ hearing matters; a rough voice that hits the right contour is enough. Singing quietly, or just hearing it internally, still counts.
