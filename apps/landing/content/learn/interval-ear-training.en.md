An interval is the distance between two notes — a minor second, a major third, a perfect fifth, an octave. If [ear training](/learn/what-is-ear-training) had a single core skill, this would be it: melodies are chains of intervals, chords are stacks of them, and every "by ear" skill musicians admire runs on interval recognition. The good news: there are only twelve of them in an octave, and they are very learnable.

## Why intervals are the core skill

When you hear a melody, what you actually perceive is not twelve individual pitches but the _shape_ made by the intervals between them — that's why you recognize "Happy Birthday" no matter what key it's played in. Nail intervals and two other skills get dramatically easier: [chord identification](/learn/chord-identification), because a chord's quality is defined by the intervals inside it, and [melodic dictation](/learn/melodic-dictation), which is just interval recognition plus memory. Train intervals first and the rest of ear training is downhill.

## The twelve intervals and their reference songs

The fastest way to anchor each interval is a song you already know that begins with it. Sing the song, and the interval comes with it:

| Interval    | Reference song (first two notes) |
| ----------- | -------------------------------- |
| Minor 2nd   | Jaws theme                       |
| Major 2nd   | Happy Birthday                   |
| Minor 3rd   | Greensleeves                     |
| Major 3rd   | When the Saints Go Marching In   |
| Perfect 4th | Here Comes the Bride             |
| Tritone     | Maria (West Side Story)          |
| Perfect 5th | Twinkle Twinkle Little Star      |
| Minor 6th   | The Entertainer                  |
| Major 6th   | My Bonnie Lies Over the Ocean    |
| Minor 7th   | Somewhere (West Side Story)      |
| Major 7th   | Take On Me (chorus)              |
| Octave      | Somewhere Over the Rainbow       |

Personalize this table. A reference song only works if the melody is burned into _your_ memory — if you don't know Greensleeves, pick a song you love that starts with a minor third and use that instead.

## Three practice methods that work

**Reference songs.** When you hear an unknown interval, mentally compare it against your anchors: "Is this the Twinkle leap? No, smaller — closer to Happy Birthday?" This builds a retrieval path from sound to name.

**Singing.** Sing the interval back before you answer, and sing intervals cold from a starting note. Singing forces your inner ear to _produce_ the distance, not just recognize it — recognition without production is fragile and collapses under test pressure. Yes, even if you think you can't sing. Especially then.

**Hear it in context.** Isolated intervals are a drill; music is the goal. Once an interval is solid alone, practice hearing it inside short melodies, and over a held bass note (a drone), which trains you to hear function, not just distance.

## A 10-minute daily routine

1. **Warm up (2 min):** sing through three or four intervals from your reference table, using a piano app to check the starting note.
2. **Drill (6 min):** answer interval questions in [Oh Your Ear](https://app.ohyourear.com)'s interval module — it replays sounds on demand and automatically collects your mistakes for review.
3. **Apply (2 min):** pick a song you like and sing its first three intervals, naming each one.

Keep it daily. Ear skills consolidate during sleep, so ten minutes every day beats an hour every Saturday. The full system for staying consistent is in [how to build a daily ear training practice](/learn/daily-practice-routine).

## Common mistakes

- **Learning all twelve at once.** Start with four or five (octave, perfect fifth, perfect fourth, major and minor thirds), get them reliable, then add two at a time.
- **Only practicing ascending intervals.** Real melodies go down too. Make sure your practice includes descending and harmonic (simultaneous) intervals.
- **Never singing.** Clicking the right answer while your inner ear stays silent builds a quiz-taking skill, not a musical one.
- **Judging sessions by score.** Some days your ear is just tired. Trend over weeks, not single sessions.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many intervals should I practice at once?

Four to five until they feel boring, then add two. Accuracy on a small set beats confusion on the full set — confusion is what you're training _out_.

### Should I learn ascending and descending intervals separately?

Train them mixed, but don't be surprised if descending is harder at first — it is for almost everyone. Reference songs exist for descending too (the start of "Joy to the World" is a descending scale).

### How long does it take to master all twelve?

With daily 10-minute sessions, most learners get reliable on the common eight within two to three months; the tritone and major seventh usually come last.

### Do I need to know the note names to answer?

No. You're naming the _distance_, not the notes. "That's a perfect fifth" requires no theory beyond the twelve names themselves.
