Chord identification is the moment ear training starts to feel like a superpower: a song plays, and instead of reaching for a chord chart you simply _hear_ that the verse sits on a minor chord and the chorus lifts to major. Like everything in [ear training](/learn/what-is-ear-training), it's a trained skill — and it has a clear method.

## Prerequisites: intervals first

A chord is a stack of intervals. A major triad is a major third with a minor third on top; a minor triad is the same stack flipped. If you can't yet hear a major third reliably, chord work will frustrate you. Spend a few weeks on [interval ear training](/learn/interval-ear-training) first — getting the thirds, fourths, and fifths solid is 80% of chord identification.

## What you're actually listening for

When musicians identify a chord by ear, they hear two things, in this order:

1. **Quality** — the chord's "color": bright and settled (major), dark and tense (minor), unstable and pinched (diminished), bluesy and unresolved (dominant seventh). Quality is emotional before it is technical, and that's fine — the emotion _is_ the signal.
2. **Root** — the note the chord is built on, usually heard most clearly in the bass. Quality tells you _what kind_ of chord; the root tells you _which_ chord.

Beginners should practice quality only, with the root ignored. Naming roots comes later and mostly rides on your interval skills.

## A three-step method

**Step 1: Learn the four triad colors cold.** Major, minor, diminished, augmented. Play or listen to each one slowly, sing its notes as an arpeggio, and attach a one-word feeling to each. Sing the arpeggio yourself — as with intervals, producing the sound is what makes recognition stick.

Anchor each color to a piece of music you already know:

| Quality      | Reference                                                  |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Major        | the accompaniment under "Happy Birthday" — bright, settled |
| Minor        | "The Godfather" theme — dark, pulled inward                |
| Diminished   | horror-film tension stingers — pinched, unstable           |
| Dominant 7th | twelve-bar blues — relaxed, but leaning somewhere          |

**Step 2: Drill pairs, not the whole set.** The confusions that matter are specific: major vs minor (the classic), minor vs diminished, major vs dominant seventh. Drill two qualities against each other until the difference is obvious, then add a third.

**Step 3: Add sevenths, then context.** Once triads are boring, add the major seventh (dreamy), minor seventh (mellow), and dominant seventh (wants to resolve). Then practice hearing quality inside short progressions — real music, where the chord lasts two seconds instead of forever.

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing the root before the quality is solid.** "It's some kind of G chord" is useless if you can't tell major from minor. Quality first, always.
- **Only listening to block chords in isolation.** Real music arpeggiates, inverts, and voices chords across instruments. Once isolated chords feel easy, switch to progressions quickly.
- **Skipping the singing.** Singing a chord's arpeggio — root, third, fifth — is the fastest way to make its color permanent. Five out-of-tune notes sung by you beat fifty perfect ones clicked by you.
- **Ignoring the bass.** When you're ready for roots, listen to the lowest note first; in most popular music it _is_ the root.

## A daily practice slot

Chord work fits into the same ten-minute frame as everything else: six minutes of quality drills, two minutes of arpeggio singing, two minutes of "name that quality" over a song you like. ## Frequently asked questions

### How long until I can tell major from minor reliably?

Most learners get there in two to four weeks of short daily sessions — it's usually the first chord skill to click. Diminished vs minor, and the seventh family, take longer.

### Do inversions matter for ear training?

Yes, but later. Inversions change the bass note without changing the quality, which is exactly why you learn quality first: it's inversion-proof. Add inversion recognition once quality is automatic.

### Should I learn chord names (C, G7) or functions (I, V)?

Start with quality, then learn functions — hearing "that's the V chord" is more useful in real music than naming absolute chords, and it's what transposition and playing by ear run on.

### Is this what I need for music school entrance exams?

Yes — chord quality and progression recognition are core exam skills. The exam-specific format and pacing are covered in [preparing for aural exams](/learn/aural-exam-prep).
