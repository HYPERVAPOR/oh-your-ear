Every guide in this series ends the same way: a few minutes of focused practice, daily. This one is about the frame itself — why daily beats long sessions, what a session should contain, and how to still be practicing three months from now, which is the only version of this that works.

## Why daily beats long sessions

Ear skills are perceptual learning, and perceptual learning consolidates during sleep — like vocabulary, not like cramming for an exam. Ten minutes every day gives your brain seven consolidation cycles a week; a one-hour weekend session gives it one. The research on spacing is about as close to unanimous as psychology gets: distributed practice wins, decisively.

There is a second reason, less noble and more practical: ten minutes is easy to start. The routine that survives is the one that survives your worst week.

## The 10-minute template

Whatever skill you're working on, the session has the same three parts:

1. **Warm up by singing (2 min).** Sing a few intervals or a scale from a reference pitch. Singing switches your inner ear on; answering questions with a cold ear wastes the session's first half.
2. **Drill your weakest skill (5–6 min).** Not your favorite one — your weakest. This is where a mistake notebook earns its keep: your confusions (major vs minor, that descending minor sixth) should set the drill, not a generic sequence.
3. **Apply to real music (2 min).** Take a song you like and do one real thing with it: name its first intervals, identify a chord quality, clap its rhythm back. Drills transfer only if you practice the transfer.

## Rotate the five skills across the week

You don't need all five skills every day. A sane weekly shape:

- **Intervals** — 3 days (it's the foundation; see the [interval guide](/learn/interval-ear-training))
- **Chords** — 2 days ([chord guide](/learn/chord-identification))
- **Melody / rhythm** — 1 day each ([melody](/learn/melodic-dictation), [rhythm](/learn/rhythm-basics))

Beginners should spend the first month on intervals alone and ignore this rotation entirely. Breadth is earned.

## Consistency tactics that actually work

- **Same time, same place.** Anchor the session to an existing habit — after morning coffee, on the commute with headphones. Motivation is unreliable; anchors aren't.
- **A minimum you can't fail.** Your rule is "every day", not "ten minutes". On a wrecked day, two minutes counts. Never zero: the streak is the asset.
- **Track the trend, not the day.** Daily scores are noisy — tired ears are real. Watch the weekly curve. If you're using [Oh Your Ear](https://app.ohyourear.com), the daily plan, streak, and mistake notebook are built exactly around this: a fixed daily quota, your errors collected automatically, progress over weeks.

## When to level up

Increase difficulty when a drill feels _boring_, not when it feels perfect — boredom means the skill is automating. Add two intervals, longer melodies, a new chord family. And re-read the first rule: the goal is still to be practicing three months from now. Difficulty that kills the habit is a downgrade, however impressive it looks.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is 10 minutes really enough?

For the first three to six months, yes — consistency matters more than volume at that stage. Later, if you're preparing for [aural exams](/learn/aural-exam-prep) or transcribing seriously, 20–30 minutes of focused work is worth it. Beyond about 30 minutes, fatigue eats the returns.

### Morning or evening?

Whenever you'll actually do it. Some studies slightly favor learning a few hours before sleep (the consolidation window), but a routine that happens beats an optimal one that doesn't.

### What if I miss a day?

Missing one day costs nothing; missing the day _after_ a miss is how routines die. The rule is never miss twice.

### Should I practice on days my ears feel tired?

Light review, yes; heavy drilling, no. Ear fatigue is real — switch to singing warm-ups or rhythm clap-backs, which are gentler, and count it as a day.
