Aural exams — the listening components of music school entrance tests, graded exams like ABRSM, and conservatory placement tests — are a different sport from casual ear training. The skills are the same ones this series covers, but the conditions are hostile: limited playings, a ticking clock, and written answers with no feedback. Training for the exam means training the conditions, not just the skills.

## What aural exams actually test

Formats vary, but the components are remarkably stable:

- **Interval and chord recognition** — played twice, name it. Foundations: [interval ear training](/learn/interval-ear-training) and [chord identification](/learn/chord-identification).
- **Melodic dictation** — a phrase played three or four times, notated. Method: [melodic dictation](/learn/melodic-dictation), whose multi-pass approach was built for exactly this.
- **Rhythmic dictation / clap-back** — reproduce a rhythm heard once or twice. See [rhythm training](/learn/rhythm-basics).
- **Sight-singing** — sing a written melody at sight. The one component that is voice-first: practice it daily, out loud, even badly.

Get a past paper or syllabus for _your_ exam early. "Aural exam" is a family, not a single test, and training to the wrong format is a classic own goal.

## The three differences that decide your grade

**Limited playings.** In practice you replay a sound twenty times; in the exam you get two to four. So ration replays in training: answer after three hearings, then check. Replay discipline is a trainable habit, and it's the single biggest gap between practice-room scores and exam scores.

**Time pressure with writing.** Dictation in an exam means decoding while the next phrase is already coming. Train with a timer, and train the _written_ answer too — many ears are fine but lose marks to slow, clumsy notation. Writing rhythm and pitch quickly is its own drill.

**No feedback.** During practice you learn from every answer; in the exam you get silence. Once a week, do a full mock with zero replays and zero checking until the end. The discomfort is the training.

## A twelve-week plan

- **Weeks 1–4: foundations.** Intervals daily (the biggest single payoff), chord qualities, two-note to five-note dictation. Unlimited replays allowed — you're building accuracy.
- **Weeks 5–8: format.** Switch to your exam's exact question types. Replays capped at three. Rhythm and sight-singing join the daily routine.
- **Weeks 9–12: simulation.** Weekly full mock under timed conditions. Error log analysis after each one: every mistake gets a name ("descending m6", "dotted rhythm"), and next week's drills target the top two names.

The daily structure throughout is the [ten-minute template](/learn/daily-practice-routine) — for exam prep, expand it to 20–30 minutes, split morning (drills) and evening (singing) if you can.

## Frequently asked questions

### How good do my intervals need to be before I start dictation?

Reliable on the common eight (unison through octave, minus the tritone and major seventh) at slow tempo. Dictation with shaky intervals builds frustration, not skill.

### My sight-singing is terrible — where do I start?

Sing scales and arpeggios to a drone or piano daily for two weeks, then single-line melodies far below your reading level. Sight-singing improves fast at first — it's the most coachable exam component.

### Should I practice with a piano timbre or varied instruments?

Whatever your exam uses — usually piano — plus occasional variation so your ear doesn't overfit to one sound.

### Can an app replace a teacher for aural prep?

For the recognition and dictation components, mostly yes: drills, replays, and mistake tracking are exactly what apps do well ([Oh Your Ear](https://app.ohyourear.com) covers intervals, chords, melody, and rhythm with unlimited replay and an automatic mistake notebook). For sight-singing feedback and mock-exam pressure, a teacher still earns their fee.
